# Post frameworks with complete examples

Eight structures that cover almost every founder post. Each has the skeleton, when to use it, and a **complete example post** you can model. Word counts are guides: most strong posts are 120–250 words.

*Example posts are illustrative, written for a fictional IT services founder. Replace every detail with real stories and numbers.*

---

## 1. Story → lesson

**Use for:** mistakes, turning points, client moments. Usually the highest comment rate.

```
[Hook: the moment, under 12 words]
[Context: 2–3 short lines: when, where, what was at stake]
[Conflict: what went wrong or what was hard]
[Turning point: the decision or realisation]
[Lesson: one sentence the reader can apply]
[Question that invites their own story]
```

**Example**

> Six years ago, I got a call at 3 am.
>
> Our biggest client's site had been down for two hours.
> Nobody on their side knew who to call. Neither did half my team.
>
> We fixed it in 40 minutes. The technical problem was small.
>
> The real problem: no runbook. No list of who does what when things break.
>
> We lost the client a month later. Not because of the outage, but because of the two hours of chaos before anyone acted.
>
> Since then, every client gets a one-page incident runbook in week one. Names, numbers, steps. Boring. Essential.
>
> Outages are rarely technology problems. They're "who's in charge?" problems.
>
> When did you learn the value of a boring process the hard way?

## 2. Contrarian opinion

**Use for:** industry beliefs the founder disagrees with. Drives reach and debate. Always be fair.

```
[Hook: the claim]
[What most people believe, and why it sounds right]
[What you've seen instead, with one specific example]
[When the common view IS right (keeps it credible)]
[Your rule of thumb]
[Question: "Where do you land?"]
```

**Example**

> Unpopular opinion: multi-cloud is mostly a sales pitch for small companies.
>
> The logic sounds great. No vendor lock-in. More resilience. Best tool for each job.
>
> But for a 100-person company, it usually means:
> → Two bills nobody fully understands
> → Two sets of security settings to get right
> → A team that's average at two platforms instead of great at one
>
> Last year we moved a client from three clouds to one. Their bill dropped 28% and incidents halved.
>
> Multi-cloud makes sense at scale, or when regulation demands it. Most mid-sized companies aren't there yet.
>
> My rule: master one cloud before you add a second.
>
> Agree, or have you seen multi-cloud work well at a smaller size?

## 3. Buyer mistake

**Use for:** helping buyers decide well. Attracts people who are actively buying.

```
[Hook: the mistake or its cost]
[Who makes it and why it's understandable]
[What it costs them, with a number or example]
[What to do instead: 3–5 steps]
[Soft CTA: save, share or DM]
```

**Example**

> The most expensive mistake buyers make with IT contracts: comparing monthly price only.
>
> It's understandable. The monthly number is the easiest to compare.
>
> But the expensive parts are usually elsewhere:
> → Onboarding fees buried in the statement of work
> → "Out of scope" hourly rates (often 2–3x the plan rate)
> → Exit fees and data-handover charges
> → Response times that only apply to "critical" issues
>
> One prospect showed me two quotes. The cheaper monthly plan was 40% more expensive over 12 months once we added realistic out-of-scope hours.
>
> Before you sign, ask every vendor for:
> 1. A 12-month total cost based on your last year of tickets
> 2. Their out-of-scope hourly rate in writing
> 3. What exit looks like, and what it costs
>
> Save this for your next renewal.

## 4. How-to / checklist

**Use for:** practical, save-worthy posts. Great as carousels too.

```
[Hook: the outcome + time or effort]
[Who it's for]
[Steps: 3–7, one line each, numbered]
[The step people skip, and why it matters]
[CTA: "Save this" or "Want the template? Comment X"]
```

**Example**

> How to read your AWS bill in 10 minutes:
>
> (For founders and finance leads, not engineers.)
>
> 1. Open Cost Explorer and group by service. Note the top 3.
> 2. Switch to "group by usage type". Look for anything you don't recognise.
> 3. Filter by "untagged" resources. That's usually where waste hides.
> 4. Compare this month to 3 months ago. Anything up more than 20%?
> 5. Check for reserved capacity you're not using.
>
> The step most people skip: number 3. Untagged resources often mean nobody owns them, and nobody switches them off.
>
> Save this for your next monthly finance review.

## 5. Client result

**Use for:** proof. Always get the client's permission, or anonymise.

```
[Hook: the result with a number]
[The client's situation, in their words]
[What you did: 3 bullets]
[What changed: numbers]
[The lesson for other buyers (not a sales pitch)]
```

**Example**

> Same servers. Half the bill.
>
> A 200-person logistics company came to us with a $28,000 monthly cloud bill. Their CFO's words: "Nobody can explain it."
>
> What we did in 30 days:
> → Tagged every resource to a team and an owner
> → Switched off 41 servers nobody was using
> → Moved steady workloads to reserved pricing
>
> Result: $14,200 a month. Same performance. No outages.
>
> The lesson isn't "hire us". It's this: if nobody owns a resource, nobody switches it off. Start with ownership, then optimise.

## 6. Myth vs reality

**Use for:** correcting misconceptions in a calm, helpful way.

```
[Hook: "Myth: ..."]
[Reality: the truth in one line]
[Why the myth exists]
[2–3 points of evidence or examples]
[What to do instead]
```

**Example**

> Myth: moving to the cloud always saves money.
>
> Reality: it saves money when you change how you run things. A straight "lift and shift" often costs more.
>
> Why the myth exists: the cloud is cheaper per unit, and the sales decks show that number.
>
> What we actually see:
> → Servers sized for peak load, running 24/7 at 10% use
> → Storage that grows forever because nobody deletes anything
> → Data transfer fees nobody planned for
>
> The cloud is a great deal if you treat it like a utility, and switch things off.
>
> Before migrating, ask: "What will we stop doing?"

## 7. List lesson

**Use for:** milestones, years in business, events. Easy to skim.

```
[Hook: "[Number] years/projects/hires. [Number] lessons."]
[Lessons: 5–10, one line each, most surprising first]
[The one that matters most, and why]
[Question: "Which would you add?"]
```

**Example**

> 10 years of running an IT company. 7 lessons.
>
> 1. Clients don't buy technology. They buy sleep.
> 2. The boring work prevents the exciting emergencies.
> 3. Never let one client be more than 25% of revenue.
> 4. Hire for curiosity. You can teach the rest.
> 5. Write it down, or it didn't happen.
> 6. Raise prices before you're desperate to.
> 7. Say no to work you'll be bad at.
>
> The one I learned most painfully: number 3. In 2017, one client was 60% of our revenue. When they left, we nearly closed.
>
> Founders: which would you add?

## 8. Behind the scenes

**Use for:** building trust and relatability. Real photos help.

```
[Hook: what you're showing]
[The scene: what happens, who's involved]
[Why you do it this way]
[What it's changed]
[Invitation: "How do you do this?"]
```

**Example**

> Every Monday at 9 am, our whole team spends 30 minutes on one question:
>
> "What almost went wrong last week?"
>
> Not what went wrong. What almost did.
>
> A backup that ran late. A certificate two days from expiring. A client who emailed the wrong person.
>
> We fix each near-miss before it becomes an incident.
>
> Since we started this in 2021, our client incidents have dropped by more than half.
>
> Near-misses are free lessons. Most teams just don't collect them.
>
> Does your team review near-misses, or only incidents?
